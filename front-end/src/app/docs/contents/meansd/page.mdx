# Mean and standard deviation (SD) conversions

In just one click, all the data types below (for one group or more) will be converted to mean and SD, and then will be automatically converted to mean and SD pre- (baseline) and post-treatment to mean and SD change.
N.B: The correlation coefficient is calculated if available or assumed if not.

## 1. Mean and standard deviation (SD) Change

### **A) Needed values**

To calculate the change in mean (x̄) and standard deviation (SD) from baseline, you need the following values:

1) Mean (x̄) (baseline)

2) Mean (x̄) post-treatment (Final)

3) Standard deviation (SD) (Baseline)

4) Standard deviation (SD) (Final)

5) Correlation coefficient (C.C)

### **B) Steps**

1) In the Input table: put each of the previous values into the corresponding cells 
2) Click submit 
3) In the Output table: you will get the mean and SD change

### **C) Equations**

The output was calculated upon the following equations: 

**1) Mean (x̄) Change:**

$$
\textbf{x̄ Change = }x̄ (Final) - x̄ (Baseline)
$$

**2) Standard deviation (σ) change:** 

$$
\textbf{SD Change = }\sqrt{\mathrm{SD}{\text { Baseline}}^{2}+\mathrm{SD}{\mathrm{} \text { Final}}^{2}-\left(2 \times \mathrm{C.C} \times \mathrm{SD}{\text { Baseline}} \times \mathrm{SD }{\mathrm{ } \text { Final}}\right)} 
$$

### D) Citation and equations source

Higgins JPT, Thomas J, Chandler J, Cumpston M, Li T, Page MJ, Welch VA (editors). *Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions* version 6.4 (updated August 2023). Cochrane, 2023. Available from www.training.cochrane.org/handbook. [Chapter: 6.5.2.8 Imputing standard deviations for changes from baseline, URL: [Chapter 6: Choosing effect measures and computing estimates of effect | Cochrane Training](https://training.cochrane.org/handbook/current/chapter-06#section-6-5-2-8)].

### E) Additional notes

**1)** In the meta-analysis, we usually need to calculate C.C if the data presented contains the following:

    A) Standard deviation (SD) (Baseline)

    B) Standard deviation (SD) (Final)

    C) Standard deviation (SD) (Change)

The output (C.C)  will be calculated upon the following equation:

$$
\textbf{C.C = }\frac{\mathrm{SD}{\text { Baseline}}^{2}+\mathrm{SD}{\mathrm{} \text { Final}}^{2}-\mathrm{SD}{\mathrm{} \text { Change}}^{2}}{2 \times \mathrm{SD}{\text { Baseline}} \times \mathrm{SD}{\mathrm{} \text { Final }}} 
$$

**2) A-** If C.C is available from more than one study, we use their average. 

     **B-** If only one C.C is reported or calculated, we use it. 

     **C-** If the previous values (SD Baseline, SD Final, and SD Change) were unavailable in all studies, C.C is assumed to be 0.50. 
  (Abrams, K. R., Gillies, C. L., & Lambert, P. C. (2005). Meta‐analysis of heterogeneously reported trials assessing change from baseline. *Statistics in medicine*, *24*(24), 3823-3844).

## 2. Mean and confidence interval (CI)

### **A) Needed values**

To estimate the mean and standard deviation (SD) from mean and 95% confidence intervals (CI), you need the following values:

1) Mean

2) Upper limit of CI

3) Lower limit of CI

4) Sample size (N)

### **B) Steps**

1) In the Input table: put each of the previous values into the corresponding cells 
2) Click submit 
3) In the Output table: you will get the data presented as mean and standard deviation

### **C) Equations**

The output was calculated upon the following equations: 

**1) SD:** 

$$
\textbf{SD = }\sqrt{\mathrm{N}} \times \frac{Upper Limit - Lower Limit }{3.92}
$$

This equation is for 95%CI. For 90% CI, 3.92 should be replaced by 3.29, and for 99% confidence intervals it should be replaced by 5.15.

## 4. Median and range

### **A) Needed values**

To estimate mean (x̄) and standard deviation (σ) from median (Md) and range (a-b), you need the following values:

1) Median (Md)

2) The smallest value [minimum in the range] (a)

3) The largest value [maximum in the range] (b)

4) Sample size (N)

### **B) Steps**

1) In the Input table: put each of the previous values into the corresponding cells 
2) Click submit 
3) In the Output table: you will get the data presented as mean and standard deviation

### **C) Equations**

The output was calculated upon the following equations: 

**1) Mean (x̄):**

$$
\textbf{x̄ = } ((a+2Md+b)/4)+((a-2Md+b)/4)
$$

    If the sample size was fairly large it can be calculated according to only:

$$
\textbf{x̄}\simeq 
 ((a+2Md+b)/4)
$$

**2) Standard deviation (σ):**  

$$
if (N\lt25) \longrightarrow  \sigma\simeq R/4\simeq \frac{b-a}{\sqrt{12}}
$$

$$
if (25\lt N\lt 60)\longrightarrow      \sigma\simeq R/4\simeq \frac{b-a}{4}
$$

$$
 if (N\gt60)\longrightarrow \sigma\simeq R/4\simeq \frac{b-a}{6}
$$

## 5. Median and inter quartile range (IQR)

### **A) Needed values**

To estimate mean (x̄) and standard deviation (SD) from median (Md) and inter-quartile range (IQR), you need the following values:

1) Median (Md)

2) First Quartile (Q1)

3) Third Quartile (Q3)

4) Sample size (N)

### **B) Steps**

1) In the Input table: put each of the previous values into the corresponding cells 
2) Click submit 
3) In the Output table: you will get the data presented as mean and standard deviation

### **C) Equations**

The output was calculated upon the following equations: 

**1) Mean (x̄):**

$$
\textbf{x̄ } \simeq  ((Q1 + Md + Q3) / 3)
$$

**2) Standard deviation (SD):** 

((Q3 - Q1) / ((2 * z^(-1) ) (0.75n - 0.125 / N+0.25))) 

where z is a constant symbol 

**Approximately (SD):** 

$$
\textbf{SD }\simeq  (Q3-Q1/1.35) = (IQR/1.35)
$$

## 6. P-value of the difference between groups

### **A) Needed values**

To estimate the mean standard deviation (SD) for each group from the mean difference and P-value of the difference between two groups, you need the following values:

1) P-value (between two groups) 

2) Sample size of Experimental group (Ne)

3) Sample size of Control group (Nc)

4) Mean Difference between two groups  

### **B) Steps**

1) In the Input table: put each of the previous values into the corresponding cells 
2) Click submit 
3) In the Output table: you will get the mean and SD (that will be used for both groups)

### **C) Equations**

The output was calculated upon the following equations: 

**1)** **P value to t value to SE then to SD**

$$
\textbf{SE = }\frac{Mean  \:Difference}{t-value} 
$$

**Then:**

$$
\textbf{SD = }  \frac{S E}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{E}}+\frac{1}{N_{C}}}} 
$$

### E) Additional notes

If the study provides you with the mean for each group, you might need to compute the mean difference using the formula: 

$$
\textbf{Mean Difference = }Mean1−Mean2
$$

Where Mean 1 is a mean for the first group, Mean 2 is a mean for the second group

When studies report levels of significance, they often use shorthand expressions like "P\<0.05" or "P=NS" (which means "not significant" and usually implies P>0.05). Instead of exact P values, a conservative approach is to round up to the nearest limit (e.g., for P\<0.05, use P=0.05; for P\<0.01, use P=0.01; and for P\<0.001, use P=0.001). However, this approach does not work when results are reported as P=NS or P>0.05 .

In a meta-analysis, you will require SDs for both the experimental and comparator groups. These equations compute the average SDs for both arms, allowing you to use this common SD for both groups.

## 9. Guidance on the Normality Assumption, Skewness Assessment, and Sensitivity Analyses

TreatMeta provides automated conversions to standard mean and SD metrics to enable synthesis in meta-analyses. However, parametric methods and quantile-to-mean transformations rely on underlying distributional assumptions. Because systematic reviewers typically have access only to published summary statistics rather than participant-level raw data, the following practical guidelines explain:
* **Evaluating Distributional Plausibility:** Practical rules of thumb using published summary statistics.
* **Conversion Sensitivity:** Categorizing which conversions depend strictly on normality versus algebraic definitions.
* **Recommended Methodological Safeguards:** Sensitivity analyses, small-sample corrections, and non-parametric alternatives.

---

### A) Assessing Distributional Plausibility from Published Summary Statistics

When individual participant data (IPD) are unavailable, reviewers can detect departures from normality using established rules of thumb applied to published summary tables:

#### Boundary and Non-Negativity Check (Altman & Bland Rule)
For inherently non-negative physical or clinical variables (e.g., hospital length of stay, intensive care unit days, laboratory biomarkers, blood loss, cost, time to recovery):
* **The Rule of Thumb:** In a normal distribution, approximately 95% of observations fall within Mean ± 2 SD, and 68% within Mean ± 1 SD.
* **Detection Criterion:**
  * If Mean < 2 SD, the normal model implies that more than 2.5% of underlying values would be negative, indicating **probable positive (right) skewness**.
  * If Mean < SD, the normal model implies that more than 16% of values would be negative, indicating **severe right skewness**. Standard mean-based conversions are heavily distorted in such cases.

#### Median vs. Mean Disparity
* In a symmetric (normal) distribution, the population mean and median coincide (Mean ≈ Median).
* When primary studies report both, or when other studies in the same systematic review report means while some report medians, check the standardized disparity:

$$
\Delta = \frac{|\text{Mean} - \text{Median}|}{\text{SD}}
$$

* If |Mean - Median| > 0.2 × SD, or if the median is substantially smaller than the mean (Mean > Median), the distribution has a pronounced right tail.

#### Quantile and Interquartile Range (IQR) Asymmetry
When a study reports the median and quartiles (Q1, Median, Q3):
* Calculate the distance from each quartile to the median:
  * D_lower = Median - Q1
  * D_upper = Q3 - Median
* **Symmetry Ratio:**

$$
\text{Ratio}_{\text{IQR}} = \frac{Q_3 - \text{Median}}{\text{Median} - Q_1}
$$

* In a symmetric normal distribution, Ratio_IQR ≈ 1.0.
* If Ratio_IQR > 1.5 (or < 0.67), there is substantial asymmetry, violating the assumption of underlying normality required for simple linear averaging.

#### Range Asymmetry Check
When minimum (a), median (m), and maximum (b) are reported:
* Check whether the median is situated near the midpoint of the range:
  * D_min = m - a
  * D_max = b - m
* A ratio (b - m) / (m - a) >> 1.0 is a classic indicator of severe right skewness, frequently observed in small clinical trials with outliers.

#### Domain Knowledge and Reporting Patterns
* **Biological and Clinical Context:** Certain biomarkers (e.g., cytokines, troponin, viral loads, D-dimer) and health economic endpoints (costs, duration of ventilation) are universally known to follow skewed (often log-normal or gamma) distributions.
* **Reporting Practices in the Field:** If original trial authors intentionally reported non-parametric summary statistics (median, IQR, or range), it is often because they tested the raw data (e.g., via Shapiro-Wilk tests) and confirmed a significant violation of normality.

---

### B) Relevance of Normality Across Conversion Types

Not all conversions in TreatMeta are equally sensitive to the normality assumption:

| Conversion | Normality Reliance | Primary Estimator / Method |
| :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Median & Range to Mean & SD** | **Very High** | Hozo (2005), Bland (2015), Wan (2014) |
| **Median & IQR to Mean & SD** | **High** | Wan (2014), Luo (2018), Shi (2020) |
| **Confidence Interval to SD** | **Moderate / Low** | Cochrane Handbook Eq. (6.5.2.2) |
| **P-value to SD** | **Moderate / Low** | Altman & Bland (2011), Cochrane |
| **Mean & SE to SD** | **None (Algebraic)** | Standard Definition: SD = SE × √N |
| **Pre-Post Change SD** | **Low to Moderate** | Cochrane Handbook Eq. (6.5.2.8) |
| **Multiple Groups Pooling** | **Low** | Cochrane Handbook Eq. (6.5.2.10) |

#### Detailed Rationale by Tier:
* **High Reliance (Quantile & Range Transformations):**
  * *Median & Range:* Sample extremes (a, b) are highly vulnerable to skewness and outlier inflation. Formulas derived under normal distribution theory produce biased estimates when distributions are skewed.
  * *Median & IQR:* Quantile models assume approximate symmetry to relate quartiles to μ and σ. Marked skewness biases the imputed mean toward the median and underestimates sample variance.
* **Moderate / Sample-Size Dependent Reliance (CI & P-value Conversions):**
  * *CI & P-value:* Relies on the sampling distribution of the mean. For moderate to large samples (N ≥ 30 to 50), the Central Limit Theorem (CLT) guarantees approximate normality of the mean estimate regardless of raw data skew. For small samples (N < 25), raw data normality is strictly required for valid t-distribution inference.
* **No Distributional Reliance (Algebraic Transformations):**
  * *Mean & SE:* Exact algebraic relationship; independent of underlying distribution.
  * *Combining Groups:* Standard weighted pooling of sums and sums of squares; robust under mild non-normality for combined cohorts.

### C) Recommended Sensitivity Analyses and Alternative Approaches

When summary statistic checks reveal evident skewness or when dealing with small sample sizes, reviewers should adopt the following methodological safeguards:

#### Recommended Sensitivity Analyses
* **Leave-Converted-Studies-Out Analysis:** Perform a sensitivity analysis comparing pooled meta-analytic effect sizes with and without studies that required quantile-to-mean conversion.
* **Subgroup Stratification by Reporting Format:** Stratify the meta-analysis by whether studies natively reported Mean ± SD versus converted from Median / IQR. If point estimates or heterogeneity (I²) differ markedly between strata, discuss the influence of skewness transparently.
* **Estimator Robustness Check:** Compare results across different conversion algorithms. For example, test Wan et al. (2014) against Luo et al. (2018) for sample mean and Shi et al. (2020) for sample standard deviation.
* **Correlation Sensitivity for Baseline Change:** If imputing change-from-baseline SDs, repeat the meta-analysis across a sensitivity grid of correlation coefficients (r = 0.2, 0.5, 0.8) to assess whether conclusion stability depends on the assumed correlation.

#### Guidance for Small Samples (N < 25 to 30)
* **Avoid Range-Based Conversions in Small Trials:** Sample range is notoriously unstable in small samples (N < 25). Prefer studies reporting IQRs over ranges whenever possible.
* **Use Sample-Size-Adjusted Estimators:** TreatMeta incorporates the optimized weightings from Luo et al. (2018) and Shi et al. (2020), which correct for small-sample finite population biases that were present in earlier formulas (e.g., Hozo et al. 2005).
* **Exact t-Distribution Degrees of Freedom:** When back-calculating SD from CIs or P-values in small studies, avoid using the large-sample normal critical value (1.96); instead, use the exact two-tailed t-distribution quantile with df = N - 1 or df = N1 + N2 - 2.

#### Alternative Synthesis Methods for Marked Skewness
If severe skewness is pervasive across the included studies:
* **Direct Median-Based Meta-Analysis:** Instead of forcing non-normal medians into means and standard deviations, synthesize medians directly using non-parametric quantile-based meta-analytic methods (e.g., McGrath et al. 2020, via the `metamedian` or `qmd` R packages).
* **Log-Transformation and Ratio of Means (RoM):** If the continuous measure is positively skewed and strictly positive (e.g., biomarker concentration, hospital stay), consider analyzing the **Ratio of Means (RoM)** or geometric means after logarithmic transformation (ln(Mean)), which stabilizes variance and eliminates skewness (Friedrich et al. 2008).
* **Qualitative or Narrative Tabulation:** If primary studies present extreme skewness and sample sizes are small (N < 15), converting medians to means may yield misleading pooled estimates. In such scenarios, reporting medians and IQRs narratively in a structured evidence table is recommended by the Cochrane Handbook.
